Salmon Rice Paper Rolls — Avocado, Carrot, Purple Cabbage

Fresh rice paper rolls filled with cooked salmon, thin-sliced avocado, julienned carrot, shredded purple cabbage, and fresh herbs — no cooking required beyond the salmon. The purple cabbage isn't just for colour: the anthocyanins (cyanidin-3-glucoside) are the same compounds as in the purple sauerkraut, crossing the blood-brain barrier with direct amyloid-β inhibition evidence relevant to APOE4. The salmon provides EPA and DHA — the most directly neuroprotective fats for APOE4 carriers. Avocado provides oleic acid and potassium (kidney stone protective). Rice paper is naturally gluten-free. Two dipping sauces with no peanuts (high oxalate) and no hoisin (contains wheat).

🟣 Why this combination works — beyond the aesthetics
🧬 Each filling is earning its place
🐟 Salmon — EPA + DHA, APOE4 priority
Wild-caught salmon has the highest EPA and DHA content of any commonly available fish. APOE4 carriers have impaired endogenous conversion of ALA (plant omega-3) to EPA/DHA — meaning dietary preformed omega-3 from oily fish is the only reliable way to maintain DHA levels in the brain. DHA is a structural component of neuronal membranes; its depletion is a mechanism in APOE4-associated neurodegeneration. Aim for wild-caught Pacific or Atlantic salmon rather than farmed when possible (higher omega-3:omega-6 ratio).
🟣 Purple cabbage — anthocyanins, same as sauerkraut
Raw purple cabbage in a fresh roll delivers the same cyanidin-3-glucoside and related anthocyanins as the fermented sauerkraut (Recipe 20), but raw and unfermented. The bioavailability profile is slightly different (fermentation increases it) but the compound is the same. The deep purple colour through a translucent rice paper wrapper is both the visual appeal you saw in the video and a literal indicator of anthocyanin density — the deeper the colour, the more there is.
🥑 Avocado — oleic acid + potassium
Avocado's fat profile is dominated by oleic acid (the same monounsaturated fat as EVOO) — the preferred fat for APOE4 lipid management. Potassium increases urinary citrate excretion, which inhibits calcium oxalate crystal formation — directly kidney-stone-protective. One quarter of an avocado across 4 rolls is a meaningful potassium dose at very low oxalate (~8–10mg/100g). The fat also buffers the GI of the rice paper.
🥕 Carrot — beta-carotene, prebiotic fibre
Carrots are very low oxalate (~5mg/100g), high in beta-carotene (converted to vitamin A — relevant for fatty liver, which impairs fat-soluble vitamin absorption), and provide inulin-type prebiotic fibre that feeds the same Lactobacillus species in your purple sauerkraut and labneh. The crunch from julienned carrot is also textually essential — it gives the roll structure and a satisfying bite.
🌯 Ingredients & Method
🧾 Ingredients — 8 rolls / 2 serves

  • 8 sheetsRice paper wrappers (22cm) — GF by nature⬜ zero
  • Fillings
  • 250–300gSalmon fillet — wild-caught preferred⬜ zero

🌯 Rice paper label check: Standard rice paper wrappers are made from rice flour, water, and salt — naturally GF with no wheat. Check the label once for your brand. Some imported brands add tapioca starch (fine) or wheat starch (not fine for Celiac). Most supermarket brands are clean.
❌ Not peanut sauce — oxalate: Peanuts contain ~187mg oxalate/100g — one of the highest-oxalate nuts. A standard peanut dipping sauce can deliver 50–100mg oxalate per serve. The tamari-ginger-lime sauce below has near-zero oxalate and does the same flavour job.
👩‍🍳 Method
  1. 1
    Cook the salmon
    Season the salmon fillet with sea salt, black pepper, and a squeeze of lime. Bake at 200°C for 10–12 minutes until just cooked through — it should flake easily but remain slightly moist in the centre. Alternatively, pan-sear skin-side down in a lightly oiled pan for 4 minutes, flip, and cook the flesh side for 2 minutes. Remove and cool to room temperature. Flake into generous pieces when cool — not too small, you want visible salmon in each roll.
  2. 2
    Prepare all fillings before rolling
    Shred purple cabbage very thinly — 1–2mm strips. Cut carrots into thin 7–8cm matchsticks. Slice avocado thinly lengthways and toss immediately in lime juice (prevents browning and adds flavour). Pick herb leaves. Lay everything out in neat separate piles on your bench before you start rolling — the rice paper softens quickly and stops being workable if you have to search for ingredients mid-roll.
  3. 3
    Make your dipping sauce(s)
    Make at least the tamari-ginger-lime sauce now. Both sauces can be made ahead. Recipe below — the cold-activated garlic goes in last.
  4. 4
    Soften one rice paper at a time
    Fill a large shallow bowl, wide pan, or baking dish with warm (not hot) water. Submerge one rice paper wrapper for 10–15 seconds — it should be pliable but still have a little resistance. It will soften further as you fill it. If it's already completely limp when you lift it out, the water is too hot or you've soaked it too long — it will tear. Lay the softened wrapper on a clean damp tea towel or damp chopping board.
  5. 5
    Fill and roll — the technique
    Place fillings in the lower third of the wrapper, not the centre:
    1
    Herbs first
    They show through — facing down
    2
    Cabbage + carrot
    Small amounts — don't overfill
    3
    Avocado slices
    2–3 slices per roll
    4
    Salmon on top
    A few generous flakes
    5
    Roll firmly
    Bottom up → sides in → forward
    Fold the bottom edge up over the filling, pulling gently to tighten. Fold in the two side flaps. Roll forward firmly — the wrapper seals itself. Place seam-side down on a plate. Don't let finished rolls touch each other — they will stick together.
  6. 6
    Serve immediately with dipping sauce
    Rice paper rolls are at their best immediately — the wrappers soften and stick over time. If making ahead, wrap each roll individually in damp paper towel, place in an airtight container, and refrigerate up to 2 hours. Do not slice until serving — slicing breaks the seal and the filling falls out.
🫙 Dipping sauces — two options
🥢 Tamari-Ginger-Lime Sauce Primary recommendation

The Asian-style sauce that matches the flavour profile of the video. Tangy, savoury, slightly spicy. Cold-activated garlic — allicin fully preserved because this sauce is never heated.

  • 3 tbsp GF tamari — not regular soy sauce (most contain wheat)
  • 1 tbsp fresh lime juice
  • 1 tsp fresh ginger, finely grated
  • 1 clove garlic — crush, rest 5 min, add cold
  • ½ tsp sesame oil
  • ½ tsp honey or maple syrup — optional, rounds the saltiness
  • pinch dried chili flakes — optional

Method: Combine everything except the garlic. Stir well. Add the rested garlic cold at the end. Taste — if too salty, add a little more lime juice and water. Makes enough for 2 serves. Keeps refrigerated for 3 days.

GF tamari vs regular soy sauce: Standard soy sauce is typically 40–60% wheat. GF tamari is brewed without wheat — same flavour, Celiac safe. Kikkoman and San-J both make clearly labelled GF tamari. Check the label — "reduced sodium" versions are also fine if sodium is a concern.
🧈 Tart Labneh Dipping Sauce When tart labneh is available

Use this when you have the sour-cream style labneh (Recipe 8 — made from older, tart yogurt). Completely different flavour direction — creamy, herby, bright. The salmon, avocado, and cucumber combination loves it. Think of it as a cultured herb cream sauce.

  • 4 tbsp tart savoury labneh — see Recipe 8
  • 1 tbsp fresh lime juice
  • 2 tbsp EVOO
  • 1 tsp fresh ginger, grated — optional
  • 1 clove garlic — crush, rest 5 min, add cold
  • 2 tbsp fresh dill or chives, finely chopped
  • pinch fine sea salt + black pepper
  • 1–2 tbsp cold water to thin to dipping consistency

Method: Whisk labneh, lime juice, EVOO, and ginger together until smooth. Add cold rested garlic and herbs. Thin with cold water, 1 tbsp at a time, until it reaches a consistency that coats a spoon lightly and drips off easily — thick enough to cling to the roll when dipped. Taste and adjust lime or salt.

Live cultures preserved: This sauce is never heated — the Lactobacillus cultures in the labneh survive entirely. The fat from EVOO and the acid from lime also slow culture die-off, so this sauce remains probiotic even after a few hours in the fridge.

🔬 Why each element is here

  • Wild-caught salmon for APOE4 — preformed DHA is the only reliable source: APOE4 carriers have a genetic impairment in the conversion of the short-chain plant omega-3 ALA (from flaxseed, walnuts) to the long-chain DHA and EPA the brain and cardiovascular system actually use. This means plant omega-3 sources are much less useful for APOE4 than for the general population. Preformed DHA from oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel) is the direct dietary solution. The evidence for DHA in slowing cognitive decline in APOE4 carriers is among the strongest dietary intervention data for this genotype.
  • Raw purple cabbage vs cooked vs fermented: Raw cabbage retains the myrosinase enzyme (same sulforaphane activation pathway as broccoli — cut and rest applies here too if you want to maximise it, though for a fresh roll the 40-minute rest isn't always practical). Raw purple cabbage delivers anthocyanins intact without the heat exposure that can reduce some flavonoid content. Fermented sauerkraut (Recipe 20) has higher anthocyanin bioavailability due to cell wall breakdown — the two formats complement each other and there's no reason to choose one over the other. Eat both.
  • GF tamari — not standard soy sauce: Tamari is a Japanese soy sauce traditionally brewed with little or no wheat. Standard Chinese-style soy sauce (including most supermarket own-brands) is 40–60% wheat by ingredient weight. For Celiac this is not a trace contamination issue — it's a major ingredient. The flavour of GF tamari is richer and less harsh than standard soy sauce; it's actually the better product for this sauce regardless of the Celiac consideration.
  • No peanut sauce — oxalate and the almond-cracker pattern: The same reasoning that removed almond crackers applies. Peanuts are ~187mg oxalate/100g and peanut sauce uses a significant quantity per serve. After a period of high dietary oxalate, adding another high-oxalate daily or regular condiment slows the return to normal urinary oxalate levels. The tamari-ginger-lime sauce provides the same flavour function at near-zero oxalate.
  • Lime not lemon for this recipe: Either works for urinary citrate benefit (both provide citric acid → excreted as urinary citrate → inhibits calcium oxalate crystallisation). Lime is the traditional pairing with Southeast Asian flavours; the flavour profile of the tamari sauce is specifically built around lime. Lemon can substitute in the labneh sauce without any change in the functional benefit.
