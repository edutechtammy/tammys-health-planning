Slow-Braised Pork Shoulder with Apple, Fennel & Thyme

Bone-in pork shoulder slow-braised low and slow with Fuji apple, fennel, onion, cold-rested garlic, and fresh thyme in a light stock. The apple dissolves into the braising liquid during the three-hour cook, creating a naturally sweet and slightly sharp sauce with no added sugar. Pork is one of the best dietary B-vitamin sources in the collection — particularly B1 (thiamine), B6, and B12 — making this the most targeted MTHFR recipe outside of leafy greens. Lean braised pork is a high-quality complete protein with negligible carbohydrate, well-suited to Dawn Phenomenon management. Fennel anethole is anti-inflammatory. The skin-on apple delivers quercetin into the sauce — same compound as Recipe 26. Garlic crushed and rested 5 minutes before adding preserves allicin. Finished with Dijon mustard and lemon off-heat.

🌿 What each ingredient is doing
🧬 Every ingredient earns its place

🍖 Pork shoulder — B1, B6, B12, complete protein

Pork is one of the richest dietary sources of thiamine (B1) — a cofactor in pyruvate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, both central to mitochondrial energy metabolism. A single 150g serve of pork provides well over the daily B1 requirement. It's also a meaningful source of B6 (pyridoxine) and B12 — both directly relevant to the methylation cycle that is impaired in MTHFR. The combination of B1 + B6 + B12 in a single meal makes lean pork arguably the most B-vitamin-dense protein in this collection outside of liver. Cut choice: shoulder provides more collagen and connective tissue than loin — this is what makes it ideal for long braises (the collagen dissolves into gelatin, giving the sauce body and the meat a yielding texture). Trim the surface fat to ~3–4mm. Total saturated fat per serve at this fat level is moderate and comparable to chicken thigh.

🍎 Fuji apple — quercetin, pectin, natural sauce body (keep the skin)

The skin of the Fuji apple is where the quercetin is concentrated — a flavonoid with direct NF-κB suppression (anti-neuroinflammatory, APOE4 relevant) and evidence for blood pressure reduction. Cooking the apple skin-on releases quercetin into the braising liquid, meaning the compound is distributed throughout the sauce. The pectin in the apple flesh dissolves during the long cook and naturally thickens the braising liquid into a sauce — no cornstarch, no flour, no added thickener. GF without any modification. The apple's natural fructose is distributed across 4 serves and the total carbohydrate impact is low, especially alongside the protein.

🌿 Fennel — anethole, gut motility, fibre

Fennel's principal bioactive compound is anethole — the compound responsible for its distinctive anise flavour. Anethole has anti-inflammatory activity via NF-κB suppression (same pathway as quercetin from the apple) and direct evidence for improving gut motility and reducing bloating. The fennel wedges become meltingly tender during the braise and absorb the pork and apple flavours. Fennel fronds (the feathery green tops) are scattered at serving — they provide a bright fresh finish and contain a concentrated form of the same volatile compounds as the bulb. Fennel is very low oxalate and provides prebiotic inulin (supports Lactobacillus).

🧄 Garlic — allicin, NF-κB suppression (crush → 5-min rest → warm, not boiling)

Allicin forms only when garlic is physically crushed or cut — the enzyme alliinase converts alliin to allicin on cell damage. The allicin formation reaction proceeds over about 5–10 minutes at room temperature. Allicin is heat-labile — immediate exposure to boiling liquid or high pan heat destroys it before it forms. The protocol here: crush the garlic, rest 5 minutes, then add to the softened vegetables in the pot over medium-low heat after turning the heat down. The garlic warms in the residual heat rather than frying. This preserves meaningful allicin despite the subsequent long braise.

🌱 Thyme — rosmarinic acid, Nrf2 activation

Fresh thyme contains rosmarinic acid — an ester of caffeic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid that has direct Nrf2 activation activity (the master antioxidant gene regulator also activated by sulforaphane in the kale and broccoli recipes). Rosmarinic acid also has evidence for inhibiting amyloid-β aggregation — directly relevant for APOE4. The compound is released into the braising liquid during cooking, distributed throughout the sauce. Fresh thyme is significantly more potent than dried, but dried works well in a long braise. Low oxalate, negligible calories, zero GI impact.

⚗️ Ingredient notes

✅ Naturally gluten-free — watch the stock and mustard labels

Plain pork, fresh vegetables, fresh apple, and EVOO are all naturally gluten-free. The two ingredients to check: stock (some commercial stocks contain gluten via maltodextrin or barley malt extract — look for "GF" or "gluten free" labelling, or make your own) and Dijon mustard (most French Dijon is GF but some contain spirit vinegar from wheat — verify the label). Everything else in this recipe is inherently GF with no modification required.

⚠️ APOE4 — lean cut, trim visible fat, use bone-in for flavour not fat

Pork shoulder has more intramuscular fat than loin or tenderloin, which is why it braises so well. For APOE4, the concern is saturated fat load. Trim the external fat cap to ~3–4mm before cooking — you need some for moisture during the long braise, but a thick fat cap is unnecessary. At a normal serve size of ~150g braised meat, the saturated fat content is moderate and well within an acceptable range for APOE4. The biggest APOE4 concern with pork is processed pork (bacon, sausage, salami, deli meats) — not lean braised cuts. Bone-in cuts are used here for flavour depth from the bone marrow, not for fat.

✅ Kidney stone — moderate purine, low oxalate, lemon at finish helps

Pork contains moderate purines (uric acid precursors) — relevant only if there is a uric acid stone component (most kidney stones are calcium oxalate, not uric acid). For calcium oxalate stones, pork is fine. The lemon added at the finish provides citrate — urinary citrate is one of the key stone inhibitors (the same reason lemon water is recommended for kidney stone prevention). The apple also adds some dietary potassium, which reduces urinary calcium excretion. This recipe has a net kidney-protective finish even with the pork included.

👩‍🍳 Method
  1. 1
    Crush garlic — rest 5 minutes before using
    Crush all 4 garlic cloves with the flat of a knife and set aside on the board. Start your other prep during this 5-minute window — it costs nothing. Allicin forms during the rest; once formed it survives brief contact with warm (not boiling) liquid. Do not add garlic during the initial sear.
  2. 2
    Trim and season the pork
    Preheat oven to 150°C / 300°F (130°C fan). Trim the fat cap on the pork shoulder to approximately 3–4mm — enough to baste the meat during the braise, not enough to create a thick layer. Pat the meat dry with paper towel — a dry surface sears better than a wet one. Season generously all over with salt and black pepper.
  3. 3
    Sear — deep golden, not burnt
    Heat the EVOO in a heavy oven-safe pot (Dutch oven or heavy casserole) over medium-high heat until shimmering. Place the pork fat-side down first and sear without moving for 3–4 minutes until deep golden. Turn and sear all remaining sides — about 12–15 minutes total. The goal is a serious crust, not a pale colour. Remove the pork and set aside. Reduce to medium heat.
  4. 4
    Soften onion and fennel
    Add the sliced onion and fennel wedges to the pot in the residual fat. Cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, for 5–6 minutes until softened and lightly golden at the edges. Add the rested crushed garlic and stir for 30–45 seconds — it should warm and become fragrant but not fry aggressively. Add the thyme sprigs and bay leaf.
  5. 5
    Add stock and apple — return pork
    Pour in the stock and stir briefly to lift any fond (browned bits) from the base of the pot. Nestle the apple quarters, skin-on, around the edges of the pot. Return the pork to the pot — the liquid should come about one-third up the side of the meat, not cover it. Bring to a gentle simmer on the stovetop.
  6. 6
    Braise in the oven — 2.5 to 3 hours
    Cover tightly with the lid and transfer to the oven. Braise at 150°C for 2.5 to 3 hours. Check at 2 hours — if the liquid has reduced significantly, add a small splash of water. The pork is done when it offers no resistance to a fork being twisted in the thickest part and internal temperature reads 90–95°C (195–205°F). The muscle fibres should pull apart with very little effort.
  7. 7
    Rest the pork — finish the sauce off-heat
    Lift the pork out of the pot and rest on a board, loosely covered with foil, for 10–15 minutes. Remove the thyme sprigs and bay leaf. The apple will have dissolved — press gently with the back of a spoon to incorporate any remaining pieces into the sauce. Off heat: stir in the Dijon mustard and the lemon zest and juice. The sauce should be well-balanced — savoury, slightly fruity, with a bright acid finish. Taste and adjust salt.
  8. 8
    Pull or slice, and serve
    Pull the pork with two forks — shoulder braised this long will pull easily. Or slice if you prefer. Arrange on plates with the fennel from the pot. Spoon the apple-thyme sauce generously over. Scatter reserved fennel fronds. Serve alongside roasted zucchini (Recipe 24), the kale salad (Recipe 26), or steamed broccolini.

🫙 Make-ahead, storage, and variations

  • Excellent make-ahead meal: Braised pork improves overnight. The sauce thickens further as it cools (from the dissolved apple pectin and collagen gelatin), and the flavours deepen. Reheat gently in the pot over low heat with a splash of water if needed.
  • Refrigerator: Keeps well for 4 days in an airtight container. The fat on the surface will solidify when cold — this is normal; it lifts off easily if you want to remove it, or stir it back in when reheating.
  • Freezer: Freezes well for up to 3 months. Portion into individual serves before freezing for easy single-meal reheating.
  • Slow cooker variation: Sear as directed, then transfer everything to a slow cooker and cook on low for 7–8 hours or high for 4–5 hours. Add the mustard and lemon at the very end, off heat. The result is very similar to the oven method.
  • Boneless shoulder or scotch fillet: Works exactly the same — reduce the braise time slightly (check at 2 hours). The bone adds depth to the braising liquid but the recipe is fully functional without it.
  • Serving suggestions: Pairs with anything in the collection — the kale salad (Recipe 26) alongside makes a complete APOE4-targeted meal. The braising sauce is good enough to eat with just steamed greens. Low-GI cauliflower mash makes a good base if you want something more substantial.

🩸 Very low GI — negligible carbohydrate in the protein portion; apple and fennel fibre in the sauce. The high protein content actively dampens the glucose response to anything else on the plate.
