Mediterranean Chop Chop Salad

A classic from the Mediterranean tradition — everything cut to the same consistent small dice so each forkful gets a complete bite of cucumber, tomato, pepper, onion, olive, and herb at once. No cooking at any stage. The EVOO dressing is doing real functional work: the fat-soluble carotenoids in the tomatoes and peppers — lycopene, beta-carotene, lutein — are absorbed 3–6× more efficiently alongside EVOO than without fat present. The garlic in the cold dressing is actually the optimal allicin format: crush, wait 5 minutes, add cold — maximum allicin preserved with zero heat degradation. Flat-leaf parsley at a full 30g is a meaningful folate dose for MTHFR. All six conditions. Ready in 20 minutes with no cooking. Naturally gluten-free with nothing to check.

🔪 The Chop Chop Principle — why the dice size matters

Every forkful should be a complete salad

The defining characteristic of a chop chop salad is uniformity: every ingredient cut to approximately the same size — roughly ½cm, about the size of a large corn kernel. This is not aesthetic fussiness. When the pieces are all the same size, every forkful contains cucumber, tomato, pepper, onion, olive, and herb together. The flavour balance that was designed when building the recipe is present in each bite rather than a mouthful of mostly cucumber followed by mostly tomato.
  • Deseed the cucumber and tomatoes — this is the most important technique step. Cucumber seed pulp and tomato gel are mostly water; left in, they dilute the dressing and turn the salad watery within 20 minutes. Half the cucumber lengthwise, run a teaspoon down the seed channel. Halve the tomatoes and squeeze gently over the sink, then scoop any remaining seeds. Takes 2 minutes and completely changes how the salad holds.
  • Soak the red onion for 10 minutes in cold water — draws out the harsh sulfur compounds. The onion that comes out is mild, slightly sweet, and doesn't overpower the whole salad. Skip this step if you enjoy assertive raw onion; follow it if you don't.
  • Garlic: crush → 5-minute rest → add cold — the cold dressing format is actually better for allicin preservation than adding garlic to a pan even with the rest time observed. No heat degradation at any point.
  • Add herbs last, labneh or feta on top only — parsley and mint bruise fast under acid. Labneh breaks down and clouds the dressing if mixed through. Both go on at the very end.
🌿 What each component is doing
🧬 Functional ingredients — each earns its place

🧄 Garlic in cold dressing — ideal allicin preservation

The allicin formation window is established throughout this collection: crush or chop, wait 5 minutes, then add. In this recipe, the garlic goes into the cold dressing and stays cold — which is actually the ideal scenario, better than adding to a hot pan even with the rest time observed. Heat above ~60°C begins degrading allicin; in a cold lemon-EVOO dressing, the allicin is fully preserved from the moment it forms until it is consumed. Allicin mechanisms: AMPK activation (reduces hepatic glucose output — Dawn Phenomenon relevant), glutathione S-transferase induction (liver detox support — MTHFR glutathione impairment compensated), inhibition of hepatic lipogenesis (fatty liver). The garlic in this dressing is doing the same therapeutic work as garlic added to a cooked dish — possibly more, because no heat is involved.

🫒 EVOO — carotenoid absorption multiplier + APOE4 direct action

This salad contains three significant fat-soluble carotenoids: lycopene (tomatoes — reduces LDL oxidation, directly relevant for APOE4 vascular risk), beta-carotene (red and yellow peppers — liver and immune support), and lutein (bell peppers and parsley — retinal and cerebral vessel protection). All three are fat-soluble: without dietary fat present at the same meal, absorption from raw vegetables is poor — typically 3–6% of available lycopene and less for beta-carotene. With EVOO at 3 tbsp, studies consistently show absorption increases 3–6×. The EVOO in this dressing is not a flavour vehicle — it is the delivery mechanism for the most therapeutically active compounds in the salad. Oleocanthal (EVOO phenolic): structurally similar to ibuprofen, inhibits COX-1 and COX-2 via the same mechanism — anti-inflammatory directly relevant to both APOE4 neuroinflammation and hepatic inflammation in fatty liver.

🍅🫑 Tomatoes + bell peppers — lycopene, vitamin C, glucose zero

Tomatoes: lycopene content is meaningful even raw (cooking increases bioavailability further, but the EVOO in the cold dressing compensates significantly). Lycopene reduces LDL oxidation — the specific form of LDL damage that APOE4 elevates risk for. Moderate oxalate deseeded (~3mg per serve); the seed gel is where most of it concentrates, which is one more reason deseeding is the right move. Bell peppers (red and yellow): among the highest vitamin C foods by weight — red pepper at 190mg/100g, yellow at 183mg/100g. Vitamin C directly enhances folate bioavailability by protecting folate from oxidative degradation (MTHFR relevant — when the conversion step is impaired, protecting the active folate that does get through matters). Beta-carotene in both colours. GI essentially zero for both.

🌿 Flat-leaf parsley — therapeutic folate dose at 30g

Flat-leaf parsley is the highest-folate common herb: 30g (the quantity in this recipe) contains approximately 57mcg folate — 14% of the RDA, meaningful as a food source especially for MTHFR where dietary folate intake matters alongside supplementation. The folate in parsley is naturally occurring (food folate, not synthetic folic acid) — it does not require the conversion step that MTHFR C677T impairs. Also provides vitamin K at a relevant dose: 30g parsley delivers approximately 246mcg vitamin K1, which complements your K2 (MK-7) supplement (K1 handles the coagulation pathway; K2 handles the calcium-directing pathway — they are complementary, not redundant). High vitamin C content further supports the folate absorption noted above. 30g is the whole herb measurement — use a generous hand. This is parsley as a functional ingredient, not a garnish.
⚗️ Notes

✅ Naturally gluten-free — nothing to check

Fresh vegetables, EVOO, lemon, garlic, dried herbs, kalamata olives, and labneh or feta are all inherently gluten-free with no cross-contamination pathway. The only label to glance at is the kalamata olives — occasionally brined in facilities that process wheat. Look for olives in brine with no preservatives other than salt and citric acid; most standard kalamata options are fine.

💡 Chickpeas — optional but a good addition as a main course

Canned chickpeas (200g rinsed across 4 serves = 50g per serve) add ~5mg oxalate per serve, keeping the total at ~16mg — still solidly low. More importantly for a main course, they add meaningful plant protein and resistant starch that further flattens the glucose response. Rinse canned chickpeas very well — the aquafaba (bean liquid) can cause gas; rinsing removes most of it. As a side dish the chickpeas are optional. As a main, include them.

✅ Labneh vs feta — both work, different roles

Feta crumbles: salty, firm, classically Mediterranean — distributes visually well and keeps the dish light. Labneh in spoonfuls: creamier, slightly tangy, adds probiotic benefit from the live Lactobacillus culture (kept cold throughout, culture remains viable). Both add calcium which is relevant for kidney stone prevention (dietary calcium binds gut oxalate before absorption). Choose based on whether you want a more traditional presentation (feta) or want the probiotic benefit from the labneh.

🔪 Method
  1. 1
    Soak the red onion
    Dice the red onion finely and place in a small bowl covered with cold water. Leave for 10 minutes while you prep everything else. Drain and pat dry before using. The harsh bite disappears; what remains is mild and slightly sweet.
  2. 2
    Make the dressing — garlic first
    Crush the garlic and set it aside for 5 full minutes — do not rush this. While it rests, combine the EVOO, lemon juice, lemon zest, oregano, and sumac if using in a small jar or bowl. After 5 minutes add the garlic. Season with salt and black pepper. Set aside.
  3. 3
    Deseed and dice the cucumber
    Halve the cucumber lengthwise. Run a teaspoon down the seed channel to scoop out and discard the seed pulp. Cut each half into ½cm strips lengthwise, then across into ½cm dice. The deseeded cucumber holds its texture and does not water down the dressing.
  4. 4
    Deseed and dice the tomatoes
    Halve the tomatoes. Gently squeeze over the sink to remove most of the seed gel, then scoop remaining seeds with a teaspoon. Dice the flesh to ½cm. Deseeding removes the watery gel — what remains is the firm, flavourful tomato flesh that holds its shape in the salad.
  5. 5
    Dice the peppers and chop the olives
    Remove the core and seeds from both bell peppers and dice to ½cm. Roughly chop the kalamata olives to a similar size — halves or thirds depending on their original size. Consistent sizing is the whole point.
  6. 6
    Combine and dress
    In a large bowl, combine cucumber, tomatoes, red pepper, yellow pepper, drained red onion, olives, and chickpeas if using. Pour the dressing over and toss well — get the EVOO coating every piece.
  7. 7
    Add herbs, then top with labneh or feta
    Add the chopped flat-leaf parsley and torn mint and toss gently. Spoon labneh in small mounds on top, or crumble the feta over. Do not mix the labneh or feta through — leave them on top. Serve immediately.
Making ahead: Prep and dress the diced vegetables (without herbs, labneh, or feta) up to 1 hour ahead and refrigerate. Add parsley, mint, and the topping only at the moment of serving. Do not dress more than 1 hour ahead — the cucumber will begin softening in the acid.

🌊 Variations and serving ideas

  • As a main course: Add the chickpeas (200g rinsed) and increase the labneh to 120g or add a soft-boiled egg. The chickpeas bring resistant starch and plant protein; the labneh brings casein protein. Together this is a complete and sustaining lunch with a very low glucose response.
  • With grilled protein: The salad works well alongside grilled salmon, chicken, or a GF flatbread as part of a larger meal. The lemon-EVOO dressing complements most Mediterranean proteins without modification.
  • Sumac — worth getting: Sumac is a ground red berry used throughout Middle Eastern cooking — intensely tangy, slightly fruity, no heat. A pinch in the dressing and a sprinkle on top transforms the flavour in a way that dried oregano alone doesn't. Available in any Middle Eastern grocery store. Low oxalate at pinch quantities.
  • Avocado addition: Diced avocado (½ per serve) integrates well and adds additional monounsaturated fat, further increasing carotenoid absorption from the tomatoes and peppers. Add at the moment of serving — avocado discolours quickly in acid.
  • Serve with GF sourdough: A slice of Recipe 1 GF sourdough with the full salad as a meal — the fibre from the bread combines with the EVOO to further blunt any starch glucose response. The ACV capsule taken when you start cooking is perfectly timed for this combination.
  • Herb variations: If mint is unavailable, fresh basil or dill both work well. Dill in particular is a classic eastern Mediterranean pairing with cucumber and labneh. The flat-leaf parsley should stay — it is doing too much functional work to be optional.
  • Pomegranate seeds: A tablespoon of pomegranate seeds scattered over the top adds visual colour, a bright tart flavour burst, and anthocyanins that complement the APOE4 anti-inflammatory work. Low oxalate at garnish quantity. Fully optional but excellent.
