Roasted Broccoli with Bone Broth Mozzarella Cheese Sauce

Broccoli chopped raw and rested 40 minutes before any heat — the myrosinase window that converts glucoraphanin to sulforaphane while the enzyme is still active. Roasted at 200°C until caramelised. The cheese sauce is built on a bone broth base thickened with arrowroot starch: part-skim mozzarella melted in at low heat, labneh stirred in off-heat for creaminess and culture preservation, finished with cold-activated garlic (allicin intact), mustard seed powder (exogenous myrosinase top-up), lemon zest, and EVOO. Comfort food rebuilt so every element earns its place.

🥦 The 40-minute rule — how sulforaphane actually forms
⚗️ Sulforaphane activation — why the rest step cannot be skipped

Sulforaphane does not exist in raw broccoli — it is produced by a reaction between glucoraphanin (the storage compound) and myrosinase (the enzyme). Myrosinase is released when broccoli cell walls are broken by chopping. It is active at room temperature but destroyed above ~40°C. Sulforaphane itself, once formed, is heat-stable up to ~140°C. The strategy is simple: let the enzyme do its work cold, then apply heat freely.

Why mustard seed powder at the finish: Some glucoraphanin survives the roasting process unconverted (the 40-min rest doesn't capture 100% of it). Mustard seed contains its own heat-tolerant myrosinase that is still partially active even after brief sauce-temperature exposure. Added at the very end off-heat, it converts the remaining glucoraphanin in the broccoli as the sauce makes contact on the plate. It's a small but measurable top-up — the same principle used in research protocols to maximise sulforaphane yield from cooked brassicas.

👩‍🍳 Method
  1. 1
    Chop and rest — 40 minutes
    Cut broccoli into florets. Chop thick stems into 1cm pieces — they contain the same glucoraphanin and are fully edible after roasting. Leave everything at room temperature for 40 minutes. Set a timer. Do not rinse with hot water, blanch, or apply any heat during this window. The myrosinase enzyme is doing its conversion work cold.
  2. 2
    Preheat oven to 200°C / 400°F
    Start the oven during the last 10 minutes of the rest period so it's fully preheated when the rest is complete.
  3. 3
    Activate both garlic cloves
    Crush 2 garlic cloves and rest both for 5 minutes. Keep them separate — one goes into the warm sauce, one goes cold into the labneh at the very end. Crushing and resting now means both are ready when the sauce comes together quickly.
  4. 4
    Roast the broccoli
    Toss the rested florets and stems with 2 tbsp EVOO, salt, and black pepper. Spread in a single layer on a large baking tray — don't crowd or the broccoli steams rather than roasting. Roast at 200°C for 20–22 minutes until the cut edges are golden and beginning to crisp and the stems are just tender when pierced. Do not stir halfway — leaving the broccoli undisturbed maximises browning on the cut surface.
  5. 5
    Build the bone broth base
    While the broccoli roasts, pour bone broth into a small saucepan over medium-low heat. Add the flavour garlic clove and nutritional yeast (if using). Dissolve arrowroot starch completely in 2 tbsp cold water — stir until no lumps remain — then pour into the broth while stirring. Keep stirring as the sauce warms and thickens. It will reach the right consistency (~coats the back of a spoon) before it reaches a full simmer. Remove from heat as soon as it thickens — you don't want it boiling when the cheese goes in.
  6. 6
    Melt mozzarella off direct heat
    With the pan off the burner or on the absolute lowest setting, add the torn mozzarella in pieces, stirring continuously. It will melt into the warm broth smoothly at this temperature. Do not return the sauce to a simmer after the mozzarella is in — heat above ~80°C causes the protein to contract and the sauce to go grainy or stringy.
    🌡️ Sauce temperature guide: The ideal window for mozzarella melting is 65–75°C — warm enough to melt fully, cool enough to stay smooth. If you have a probe thermometer, this is worth checking the first time. If not: the broth should be steaming but not bubbling.
  7. 7
    Off-heat finish — labneh, cold garlic, mustard seed, lemon zest
    Remove pan completely from heat. Add the labneh and the second cold garlic clove (allicin preserved) and stir through until the sauce is smooth and creamy. Add ¼ tsp mustard seed powder, 1 tsp lemon zest, the EVOO drizzle, and black pepper. The sauce should go over the broccoli immediately while still warm — it thickens as it cools.
  8. 8
    Plate and serve
    Arrange the roasted broccoli on a warm plate or shallow bowl. Pour the cheese sauce over generously — it will pool slightly at the base and coat the florets. Serve immediately. The mustard seed myrosinase begins converting any remaining glucoraphanin in the broccoli on contact.
🔄 Variations
As a main — add protein
Serve over or alongside the Herb-Crusted Chicken (Recipe 13) or sliced kefir-marinated chicken breast. The cheese sauce acts as the shared sauce for both components. Add a slice of the Olive Bread (Recipe 11) to make it a complete meal.
Broccoli + cauliflower mix
Cauliflower is very low oxalate and applies the same sulforaphane logic (also a brassica, same glucosinolate chemistry). Use half broccoli, half cauliflower — same 40-min rest applies to both. The cauliflower absorbs the cheese sauce particularly well.
Kefir-milk mozzarella version
Use the kefir-milk mozzarella (20–25% kefir, see Recipe 16) in the sauce for a tangier, more complex flavour. The lactic tang from the kefir mozzarella plus the labneh creates a noticeably richer sauce.
No mozzarella — full labneh sauce
If mozzarella is not made yet: use 4–5 tbsp labneh total, all stirred off-heat into the bone broth thickened base. The sauce will be thinner and tangier but fully functional. Add an extra tbsp nutritional yeast to compensate for the reduced body.

🔬 Why each element is here

  • 40-minute raw rest — sulforaphane is not negotiable: Sulforaphane has some of the strongest evidence of any food compound for liver protection — it directly activates Nrf2, the master regulator of cellular antioxidant response, and is under active investigation for APOE4/neurological protection. But it doesn't exist in broccoli; it's produced by an enzymatic reaction that only works cold. The 40-minute rest is the entire mechanism. Skipping it and just roasting broccoli produces a much lower sulforaphane yield — essentially standard roast vegetable without the therapeutic upside.
  • Roasting over steaming or simmering: Simmering broccoli in water leaches water-soluble compounds (including the sulforaphane that did form, and the folate relevant to MTHFR) directly into the cooking water, which is then discarded. Roasting keeps all of these in the vegetable. The Maillard browning is also a genuine flavour advantage — roasted brassica has a nutty depth that steamed broccoli doesn't, which matters for eating compliance.
  • Bone broth as the sauce base: Glycine and proline from the collagen in bone broth support hepatocyte glutathione synthesis (Fatty Liver, MTHFR) and gut barrier repair (Celiac). The natural glutamate provides umami depth without added MSG or salt. Using bone broth instead of milk as the sauce base also avoids stacking casein on top of the labneh and mozzarella — the casein load stays lower, which matters for chlorogenic acid absorption if this dish follows or precedes coffee.
  • Arrowroot or tapioca starch — not flour: Both are Celiac-safe (naturally GF, no cross-contamination risk if labelled GF), extremely low oxalate, and have a lower glycaemic impact than corn starch or wheat flour at equivalent thickening doses. Arrowroot produces a cleaner, slightly glossy sauce and tolerates gentle acid (lemon) better than corn starch, which can thin when acidified.
  • Labneh off-heat — cultures and tang: Adding labneh to a boiling sauce kills the live cultures that justify using it over sour cream or crème fraîche. Off-heat addition preserves them. The tang from the lactic acid also brightens the entire sauce in the same way a squeeze of lemon would — this is why both labneh and lemon zest go in at the finish rather than during cooking.
  • Mustard seed powder at finish — not during cooking: The myrosinase in mustard seed has better heat tolerance than broccoli's own myrosinase but still loses significant activity above ~60°C. Adding it off-heat to the finished sauce, which then contacts the warm broccoli on the plate, provides the best conversion environment for any remaining glucoraphanin. ¼ tsp is a small amount — it does not taste strongly of mustard; it reads as background warmth.
  • Lemon zest over lemon juice in the sauce: Lemon juice added to an arrowroot-thickened sauce can thin it slightly (the acidity begins to break down the starch network over time). Lemon zest delivers all the aromatic citrus compounds and citrate benefit with zero impact on sauce texture. Both contribute urinary citrate for kidney stone prevention.
